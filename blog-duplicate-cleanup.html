A deliberate duplicate-cleanup workflow

Duplicate detection is a probability problem. Merging is a data-governance decision. Treating those as separate stages produces safer, more explainable cleanup.

1. Start broad and read-only

Use Duplicate Radar to see which records have the most likely duplicates, or investigate one contact. This establishes the size and shape of the problem without modifying any record.

2. Define what a match means

Names, email addresses, telephone numbers, and business identifiers do not have equal reliability. A reusable match profile should state which fields are exact or fuzzy, how much each field contributes, the minimum confidence, and which fields are required. Test profiles on a sandbox sample before applying them to a larger dataset.

3. Separate candidates from decisions

A high score is not approval to merge. Open both records, verify relationships and activity, review conflicting values, and choose the survivor deliberately. Record why the survivor is authoritative instead of defaulting to the newest or oldest record without context.

4. Put limits around automation

Auto-merge rules should be environment-specific. A sandbox can tolerate more experimentation than production. Use minimum confidence, maximum conflict count, protected fields, blank-fill policy, and maximum cluster size to force ambiguous cases into manual review.

Do not confuse scheduling with unattended merging. Dynamic Dynamo Kit recurring scans export CSV findings for review; they do not automatically merge records.

5. Keep evidence and recovery options

Export scan results and merge decisions. Keep an independent Dataverse backup before merges. If the toolkit reports an action as undoable, that is an additional safety net, not a substitute for a restore point.
